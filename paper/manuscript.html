When Does Recalibration Help Cellular Throughput Commitments? Accounting for Cost, Missingness, and Abstention

Md. Moazzem Hossain Majumder

Abstract

Calibration policies evaluated at the same nominal query cap can mislead through unequal probe spending, unobserved outcomes, and heavy abstention. Across 16 physical campaign-dates pooling retrospective multi-carrier archives, matched-cost supported-throughput differences between Missingness-Aware Recalibration under Uncertainty and Risk (MARUR) and periodic recalibration are statistically indistinguishable from zero. Across mobile cellular cohorts under frozen tree predictors, positive-offer upper violation risk averages roughly 17–63%—far above nominal α=0.10\alpha=0.10—while supported measured throughput remains only 1–3% of observed rate. We establish an auditable empirical accounting framework separating realized probe attempts, finite-record missing-outcome risk intervals, selective offer decomposition R=(M/N)R+R=(M/N)R_+, and physical cluster inference, benchmarking MARUR against Fixed-Period recalibration (Periodic) and Horizon-Paced Selective-Offer Conformal Control (PACER) across retrospective and held-out public archives. Exploratory sensitivity analyses across conformal windows, nominal error levels, and alternative source-only predictors confirm these trade-offs, while live prospective field trips remain absent.

Introduction

An application that commits to a minimum cellular throughput needs both useful delivered bandwidth and a tolerable rate of broken promises. Point forecast error, low all-issued average violations, and equal nominal recalibration budgets do not independently establish either property. Under sparse feedback, a calibration policy can appear safer simply by abstaining more often, avoiding expensive probes, front-loading its query budget before distribution shifts arrive, or omitting unmeasured horizon outcomes from its failure rate.

This paper asks a concrete empirical measurement question: when does recalibration feedback help cellular throughput commitments, and how do unequal query cost, missing responses, and abstention make apparent gains misleading? Rather than claiming a universally superior conformal controller, our central contribution is an auditable evaluation and accounting framework tested across six retrospective cellular cohorts and four held-out public archives. Specifically, we contribute:

  1. Causal acquisition accounting: An event-ledger methodology separating initial pre-test labels from charged new probes, unsuccessful horizon completions, residual-window admissions, and delayed availability for subsequent forecasts.

  2. Selective-risk and missing-outcome bounds: Exact finite-record bounds [e/M,(e+m)/M][e/M,(e+m)/M] alongside the identity R=(M/N)R+R = (M/N)R_+, demonstrating how all-issued reliability conceals conditional failure rates of roughly 1717–63%63\% under heavy abstention.

  3. Physical-cluster and matched-cost inference: Pairing policies at equal realized probe expenditures over physical campaign-dates (G=16G=16 retrospective dates; G=26G=26 held-out dates), showing that large configuration grids (576576–1,8481{,}848 runs) do not multiply independent statistical evidence.

  4. Honest comparator and bound auditing: Benchmarking Missingness-Aware Recalibration under Uncertainty and Risk (MARUR) and Horizon-Paced Selective-Offer Conformal Control (PACER) against established baselines, alongside algebraic limits showing where sparse-budget guarantees become vacuous and where controllers should not be deployed.

All evaluations use retrospective and frozen held-out public archives; zero prospective live field trips have been collected, and all negative findings are retained.

Related Work

Cellular Measurement, Prediction, and Rate Decisions

Operational measurements establish that cellular throughput depends on radio conditions, mobility, and the collection regime. The channel/context dataset of Raca et al. (Raca et al. 2020) and the commercial mmWave measurements underlying Lumos5G (Narayanan et al. 2020) support context-aware prediction. More recent multi-technology measurements, longitudinal service analysis, and machine-learning modeling extend this evidence to evolving non-standalone networks (Kousias, Rajiullah, Caso, Ali, et al. 2024; Caso et al. 2024; Kousias, Rajiullah, Caso, Alay, et al. 2024). However, forecast counts within a shared route or campaign are temporally correlated rather than independent replications, and point error does not measure commitment violation risk.

Missing predictors and deployment constraints motivate complementary approaches. BAMMO incorporates missing covariates into additive boosting on commercial 5G data (Jacobson et al. 2025), addressing missing inputs rather than unsuccessful acquisition of future calibration outcomes. Real-time smartphone evaluations highlight sampling, labeling, and distribution shift (Raca et al. 2024), while multi-carrier experiments reveal conflicting histories that limit fine-grained accuracy (Basit et al. 2024). The closest work to our decision objective is Sirhan’s evaluation of global, regime-conditioned, and adaptive conformal throughput intervals (Sirhan 2026), which shows that mobility conditioning can aid two-sided intervals while a global one-sided bound performs competitively for rate selection. Neither work audits how unequal probe spending, unsuccessful responses, and heavy abstention interact across budgets and physical campaigns.

Missingness, Adaptation, and Budgeted Feedback

Conformal calibration under missing covariates distinguishes marginal coverage from missingness-pattern conditioning (Zaffran et al. 2023). Covariate-shift weighting (Tibshirani et al. 2019) and non-exchangeable weighted conformal methods (Barber et al. 2023) establish bounds under structural assumptions that break under unrestricted temporal drift and outcome censoring. Time-series conformal methods (Zaffran et al. 2022; Xu and Xie 2023; Lin et al. 2022; Gibbs and Candès 2024), online level adaptation (Gibbs and Candès 2021; Bhatnagar et al. 2023; Angelopoulos et al. 2023; Anastasios Nikolas Angelopoulos et al. 2024; Podkopaev et al. 2024), and risk-controlling formulations (Bates et al. 2021; Anastasios N. Angelopoulos et al. 2024; Gibbs et al. 2025; Gauthier et al. 2026) establish strong full-feedback baselines, while selective classification (Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017) and stability critiques (Min et al. 2026) formalize the link between abstention and conditional error.

Budgeted and partial-feedback calibration has recently gained direct attention. Ilyas et al. (Ilyas et al. 2026) analyze upfront stopping rules for i.i.d. arrivals, Ge et al. (Ge et al. 2025) study stochastic semi-bandit feedback revealed by set inclusion, and Hou et al. (Hou et al. 2025) calibrate probabilistic solvers via sporadic edge–cloud feedback. Our empirical contribution bridges these threads inside real cellular logs by accounting jointly for charged measurement probes, failed responses, selective offer frequency R=(M/N)R+R = (M/N)R_+, and physical collection-date dependence across established reactive (MARUR) and paced (PACER) comparators. Decision Process and Accounting Framework

Scheduled Opportunities and Acquisition Ledger

At scheduled issue time tt, a frozen source-trained predictor outputs point forecast ptp_t using only telemetry and lagged throughput measured strictly before issue. The controller issues a nonnegative throughput commitment Lt≥0L_t \ge 0 and decides whether to request the future outcome yty_t over the measurement horizon (ready timestamp). All scheduled intervals enter the decision ledger; ticks lacking a valid prior rate are marked ineligible (Lt=0L_t = 0).

Every policy begins with identical historical pre-test labels (ten complete initial calibration rows per cohort), whose prior physical acquisition attempts are unknown and accounted separately from new target queries QnewQ_{\text{new}}. Each new feedback request is charged against budget CnewC_{\text{new}} at issue time, including queries whose responses arrive missing (unsuccessful), time out, or mature after the evaluation stream ends. The ledger logs five distinct quantities: issued requests QnewQ_{\text{new}}, completed observed responses, failed responses, residuals admitted to the calibration buffer, and updates actually available before later issue times. Because raw lag-throughput monitoring and oracle evaluation labels remain outside the calibration query budget, fewer requested calibration labels do not establish physical energy or radio byte savings. Unmatched operating points where policies consume unequal realized queries are never interpolated into equal-cost claims.

Evaluated Controllers and Baselines

We evaluate nine policy families under a common causal interface (Table II; implementation details and the MARUR-vs.-PACER decision taxonomy appear in Supplementary Table S14):

  • Static and nonadaptive baselines: Frozen (Cnew=0C_{\text{new}}=0, initial 10 labels only), Early (queries every eligible tick until cap exhaustion), Random (pre-drawn uniform schedules over seeds {42,123,2026}\{42, 123, 2026\}), and Periodic (fixed spacing every P=15P=15 ticks).

  • Online sparse-feedback comparators: Adapted ACI (Gibbs and Candès 2021) (η=0.05\eta=0.05, updating αt\alpha_t conservatively on missing requested responses with Lt≤ptL_t \le p_t) and DecayingOGD (Anastasios Nikolas Angelopoulos et al. 2024) (step exponent 0.60.6). Neither retains full-feedback theoretical assumptions under delayed selective censoring.

  • MARUR (reactive comparator) and ablations: Triggers probes on 60-minute response age or LTE/NR pattern switches, maintains a FIFO buffer (W=10W=10) inserting conservative upper residual ptp_t when missing, requires at least nine matching-pattern residuals for pattern quantiles (otherwise pooled), and abstains below three matching entries. Note failed responses still reset the cooldown clock even when an ablation drops missing scores.

  • PACER (paced comparator): Formulated after inspecting MARUR failures on the retrospective cohorts, PACER divides a finite horizon TT into K=min⁡(Cnew,T)K = \min(C_{\text{new}}, T) contiguous blocks, draws one uniform probe per block, excludes missing responses from an observed residual buffer (Wobs=20W_{\text{obs}}=20), updates its threshold aka_k only on positive offers via smooth interpolation/veto (aveto=0.15a_{\text{veto}}=0.15), and freezes thresholds within each block.

Retrospective Evaluation and Physical Cluster Inference

Cohorts, Dependency Structure, and Predictor Choice

The retrospective benchmark spans six cohorts evaluated at 60-second causal intervals (Kousias, Rajiullah, Caso, Ali, et al. 2024; Caso et al. 2024; NUWiNS 2025; Raca et al. 2020): Rome E1 (2023 Op1) and E3 (2023 Op2), US Drive 3 (AT&T, T-Mobile, Verizon), and Ireland driving downloads. To avoid temporal or cross-carrier leakage across chronologically ordered partitions, all five retrospective splits of earliest Rome Op1 (Zenodo 8224890) reserve distinct calendar dates (35/10/15/15/25% of days), leaving exactly 57 causal training rows (two dates), 9 validation rows, and 33 source-calibration rows for the frozen Histogram Gradient Boosted Machine (HistGBM) predictor. While a 57-row fit exhibits high cross-domain point violation rates (5757–79%79\% across held-out suffixes), Section 6 verifies that replacing it with a lower-MAE neural predictor trained on the same split (ThroughputResNet), a pinball quantile regressor, a 112-row clock-minute source tree, or raw lag Persistence leaves every accounting conclusion intact.

Critically, evaluating multiple carriers during the same drive (three carriers sharing US Drive 3; Op1 and Op2 sharing six of seven Rome collection dates) or running large parameter sweeps does not increase independent sample size. Pooling all six retrospective cohorts yields only three distinct geographic campaigns and G=16G=16 physical campaign-dates: 8 Rome dates, 3 shared US dates, and 5 Irish driving dates spanning 11 traces.

Limitations, Reproducibility, and Scope

Threats to Validity: (1) Internal: Random baselines use full-horizon eligibility offline; prospective runs require pre-committed schedules. Fixed 60-second clock intervals differ from transport-level transfer completions, and position-based QueryCensor20 does not replicate correlated physical radio outages. (2) External and statistical: All ten evaluated cohorts come from historical public archives; prospective_trips_collected remains 00. Frozen artifact frozen_rome_histgbm.joblib and harness scripts/run_prospective_validation.py remain ready without modification, alongside an empty prospective pilot template in the supplement, should live trips be collected later. (3) Construct and secondary scope: Results strictly evaluate cellular throughput commitment accounting; session boundary sensitivity, random-split trace analyses (Raca et al. 2020; Sirhan 2026), trace-driven Oslo video QoE replays, and the detailed MARUR-vs.-PACER comparator taxonomy reside in the accompanying supplementary document.

Reproducibility and Hardware: Locked configurations, SHA-256 checksums, and MIT-licensed scripts run end-to-end on standard x86-64 CPUs in Python 3.11 (verified in a fresh virtual environment via scripts/reproduce_all.py with CPU fallback for the optional ThroughputResNet neural comparator, which also supports CUDA 12.1 / RTX 4060). Continuous integration runs CPU unit tests alongside automated manuscript-table verification and manifest digest checks.

Conclusion

Evaluated rigorously over 1616 retrospective and 2626 held-out physical campaign-dates, sparse recalibration feedback does not automatically improve cellular throughput commitments. Matched-cost cluster utility differences between MARUR and Periodic are statistically indistinguishable from zero (−0.09-0.09 pp equal-date mean), conditional upper violation risks routinely span 17%17\%–63%63\% far above α=0.10\alpha=0.10, supported throughput generally remains 1%1\%–3%3\%, and favorable pooled risk headlines (such as −23.41-23.41 pp at cap 10) can hinge on offer-count weighting across heterogeneous cohorts while vanishing under equal-cluster weighting (+0.37+0.37 pp) or higher query caps. Separating realized costs, missing-outcome bounds, offer frequency, and cluster units—while recognizing when theoretical bounds hold trivially—prevents misleading deployment claims until genuinely prospective field validation becomes available.
